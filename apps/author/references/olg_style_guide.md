# OLG Writing Style Guide

This guide defines how to write content for the Online Guide (OLG). It combines rules from the OLG Writer's Manual with observed patterns from actual OLG pages written by policy staff.

## Voice and Tone

**Write directly to the reader in second person.** The reader is an agency caseworker — an eligibility worker, case manager, FAW, or supervisor — who needs clear, actionable guidance. Address them as "you."

- "You must send a VC-1" (correct)
- "A VC-1 must be sent" (incorrect — passive voice)
- "Case managers should send a VC-1" (incorrect — not direct)

**Be professional but accessible.** The OLG is not a legal document. Translate policy into plain, practical language that a worker can act on immediately. Avoid legal phrasing, but be precise about requirements.

**Use "must" for requirements.** Never use "shall" or "should" when something is required. "Must" is the OLG standard for mandatory actions. Use "may" for optional actions and "can" for ability. Reserve "will" for describing system behavior or what happens automatically — never use "will" as a directive to the reader.

- "You must verify identity before issuing expedited benefits" (requirement — directive to the reader)
- "You may contact the client's employer to verify income" (optional action)
- "You can view past SVES requests in the Tools tab" (ability)
- "BEACON will generate an automated VC-1 two business days after the interview" (system behavior — not a directive)
- "Front end staff will keep a list..." (incorrect — use "must" if it's a requirement)

## Page Structure

Every OLG page follows a predictable structure. Not every page needs every element, but the order is consistent:

### 1. Title
Clear, descriptive page title. Use the topic name, not a question or action phrase.
- "Verifying Social Security Numbers" (correct)
- "How Do I Verify SSNs?" (incorrect)

### 2. Opening Paragraph
One to two paragraphs that establish context: what this topic is, why it matters, and who it applies to. This orients the reader before they get into details.

Examples of effective openers:
- "The verification of Social Security Numbers (SSN) is a critical, mandatory requirement for all applicants seeking eligibility for public-assistance programs."
- "To better serve our clients, the Department provides the option to conduct agency business with virtual meetings through Zoom."
- "Clients may contact a Local Office directly by calling the main line to request services or information related to agency business."

Pattern: State the topic, its importance, and its scope in one or two sentences. Then provide any essential background the reader needs before proceeding.

For policy-heavy topics, it may be appropriate to cite the regulatory authority (CMR, federal regulation) in the opening to establish the legal basis for the requirement. This is not required for every page, but is helpful when the regulatory grounding strengthens the guidance — especially for topics where compliance is being emphasized. Use the writer's judgment or follow the requester's direction.

### 3. Policy Sections
Explain the rules, requirements, and standards. Use Heading 2 for major sections, Heading 3 for subsections.

When explaining policy:
- State the rule clearly and directly
- Identify who it applies to
- Note any thresholds, exceptions, or conditions
- Explain the "why" when it helps the reader understand and remember

### 4. Procedures (BEACON Steps)
Step-by-step instructions for carrying out the policy in BEACON. Use numbered lists.

Format for BEACON steps:
- Start each step with a lowercase verb: "click", "go to", "select", "enter", "open"
- Be specific about screen elements: "click the Narrative tab" not "navigate to narratives"
- Do not use "on" with "click": "click Save" not "click on Save"
- No uppercase letters or parentheses after numbers
- Insert a Note or Important callout between steps when a critical warning applies mid-procedure

### 5. Special Circumstances / Edge Cases
Address common complications, exceptions, and unusual situations. Workers encounter these regularly and need guidance without having to ask a supervisor.

### 6. Cross-References
Link to related OLG pages wherever they are relevant. The format is flexible — use the writer's judgment for what fits the context:

- `See: Page Title` on its own line (most common — clean, scannable)
- `See also: Page Title` for supplementary references
- `For more information, see Page Title` inline when it flows naturally in a sentence

The key rules: cross-references must always be present where relevant, and they must be consistently executed within a page. Do not omit them — readers rely on them to navigate the guide.

**Cross-references go in two places:**
1. **Inline, after the relevant section** — so readers exploring a specific topic find related content immediately
2. **In the Related Topics section at the bottom** — so readers scanning the page can see all connections at a glance

Both placements serve different reading patterns. Use both.

### 7. Related Topics
A summary section near the bottom of the page listing all related OLG pages. Use Heading 2 for the section title. List each related page as a separate line. This section collects the cross-references that also appear inline throughout the page, plus any additional related pages.

### 8. Last Update
The final element on every page. Format: "Last Update: Month Day, Year" (e.g., "Last Update: April 1, 2026"). Use Heading 3 style so it appears in the quick links sidebar.

## Content Principles

### Policy Before Procedure
Always explain the rule before explaining how to carry it out. The worker needs to understand *what* they're doing and *why* before they learn *how*.

### Action-Oriented Writing
Every section should leave the reader knowing what to do. Even informational sections should end with guidance on what action the reader should take with the information.

### Explain, Don't Just State
Don't just list rules — explain them enough that the reader can apply them correctly in edge cases. A well-written section gives the worker enough understanding to handle situations the page doesn't explicitly cover.

Example (good): "Clients are not required to enter an SSN when submitting an application. If the client cannot be reached by cold call, complete the web application workflow. Verification of SSN through SVES will be part of the standard processing."

Example (weak): "SSN entry is not required at application."

### Chunk Detailed Content
If a section covers multiple items or scenarios, break it into subsections with their own Heading 3 headers rather than writing one long section. Workers scan for their specific situation — make it easy to find.

Use proper heading levels (Heading 2, Heading 3, Heading 4) for all sub-sections — not bold Normal text. Headings appear in the sidebar quick links, which helps readers navigate directly to the section they need.

### Use the "What it means / What to know / What to do" Pattern
For reference sections that explain different categories or statuses, this three-part structure works well:
- **What it means**: Define the term or status
- **What to know**: Explain implications and context
- **What to do**: Give the specific action to take

## Formatting Rules

### Text Formatting
- **Bold**: Use sparingly for emphasis in body text. Bold is primarily for titles and subtitles (headings). When used inline, it should highlight a critical word or phrase, not entire sentences.
- **Italics**: Use only for the names of forms and brochures.
- **Quotation marks**: Use only for direct quotes or exact text the reader should say or type. Do not use for emphasis.
- **Screen elements**: Write button names, tab names, and field names in plain text without special formatting. "Click Save" not "Click 'Save'" or "Click **Save**."

### Quoting Exact Text and Scripts
When providing exact text that workers should say, type, or enter into a system field, use quotation marks. This distinguishes the exact text from instructional prose.

- For short scripts or phrases: use inline quotation marks within the paragraph
- For longer templates (multi-line narrative templates, structured data entry): present as a formatted block, clearly set apart from the surrounding instructions

Note: Conventions for quoting user-entered text (such as VC-1 scripts) are still being developed. When writing pages that include exact text for system entry, be deliberate and consistent in how quoted text is presented. This convention will be refined as more examples are produced.

### Lists
- **Numbered lists**: Use for BEACON steps and any sequential procedure. Steps start with lowercase verbs.
- **Bullet lists**: Use for non-sequential lists (items to check, types of documents, examples).
- Do not mix numbered and bulleted lists within a single sequence.

### Callout Boxes (Note / Important / Reminder)
Use the Note style for critical information that supplements the main text. Start with a bold label:

- **Note:** For supplementary information the reader should be aware of
- **Important:** For warnings or critical requirements that could cause errors if missed
- **Reminder:** For reinforcing something the reader already knows but might forget in this context

Keep callout text to one paragraph. Two paragraphs maximum for complex warnings. Do not overuse — if everything is "Important," nothing is.

### Tables
Use simple tables for structured reference information (codes, categories, comparisons). Bold the header row. Keep cell content concise.

### Acronyms
Spell out on first use, followed by the acronym in parentheses. Use the acronym only for subsequent references within the same page.
- First use: "Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP)"
- After: "SNAP"

## Terminology

### Standard Terms
- Clients are **"clients"** — never "customers," "recipients," or "beneficiaries"
- Use **"household members"** for people in the assistance unit
- The case-management system is **"BEACON"** — always capitalized
- Use **"case manager"** or **"FAW"** (First Available Worker) for the worker role
- The local office is a **"TAO"** (Transitional Assistance Office) or **"Local Office"**
- Use **"they/them/their"** as gender-neutral singular pronouns

### Words to Avoid
- "shall" — use "must"
- "should" — use "must" for requirements, "may" for optional actions
- "at the present time" — use "now" or "currently"
- "in order to" — use "to"
- "utilize" — use "use"
- "prior to" — use "before"
- "subsequent to" — use "after"

## Page Types

Different topics call for different page structures. Here are common patterns:

### Policy + Procedure Pages
The most common type. Explain the rule, then explain how to carry it out.
Structure: Opening context → Policy rules → BEACON procedures → Edge cases → Cross-references

### Procedure-Heavy Pages
For topics that are primarily about how to do something in BEACON or follow a workflow.
Structure: Overview → Step-by-step procedures → Special cases → FAQ (if applicable) → Cross-references

### Reference / Guide Pages
For topics that explain how something works rather than prescribing action.
Structure: Explanation with examples → Visual aids → Tips → Cross-references

### Overview / Landing Pages
For topics that introduce a broader section and link to detail pages.
Structure: Brief summary → Sections for each sub-topic with "See:" links → Related Topics

### Consolidation Pages
For gathering scattered content from multiple pages into one reference.
Structure: Introduction explaining purpose → Content organized by theme or program → Cross-references to source pages
